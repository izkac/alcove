Removable drive drawer

Plug a USB stick in and it opens as a drawer on the desk.

TL;DR

Today, plugging in a USB stick gets you the Windows Autoplay dialog and then an Explorer window sitting on top of the desk Alcove already owns. After this change the stick opens as a drawer, in the same place and the same shape as everything else, with an eject button in its header. Pull the stick and the drawer goes away by itself.

What you'll get

  • A drawer appears within about two seconds of plugging in a USB stick or a memory card, named after the volume's label.
  • It behaves like any other folder drawer: browse it, drill into subfolders, drag files out into an Alcove, open something.
  • An eject button inside the drawer. It flushes and unmounts the drive, then closes the drawer.
  • Unplugging without ejecting also closes the drawer — no ghost drawer left pointing at a drive that isn't there.
  • Nothing is remembered. Restarting Alcove never resurrects a drawer for a stick you used last week.
  • A switch in Settings turns the whole thing off. It's on by default.

How it works

Alcove already has "live folder" drawers — a drawer that mirrors a folder on disk. A USB stick is just a folder at E:\, so the drawer itself is free. What's new is noticing the drive. Rather than subscribing to Windows device events, Alcove reuses the two-second heartbeat it already runs to spot changes on the Desktop, and asks "which removable drives are mounted right now?" on the same tick. The answer is compared to the drawers on screen, and drawers are added or removed to match.

What changes for you

  • Windows Autoplay still pops up — Alcove does not touch that system setting. The drawer is a better place to go than the dialog, not a replacement for it. Turn Autoplay off in Windows Settings if you want it gone.
  • A new Settings row: open a drawer for USB drives.
  • Removable drawers can't be renamed, recoloured or kept — they're temporary by design.

Risks and open questions

  • Eject may not remove the drive letter. Alcove does the part that protects your data — flush and unmount — but on some sticks the letter stays in Explorer until you physically pull it. The full "safely remove hardware" behaviour needs a lot more code and was deliberately deferred.
  • A drawer opening unasked is intrusive. That's what the off switch is for. The drawer will not steal focus from a canvas you have open.
  • Slow drives. A card reader with no card, or a drive spinning up, can be slow to answer. Reading the drive list happens off the main thread, and a drive that won't give up its label is shown as "Removable Disk".
  • Testing needs real hardware. The logic is covered by automated checks, but the final "plug it in, see the drawer" pass is manual.

Out of scope

  • Suppressing or replacing Windows Autoplay.
  • Phones and cameras that connect over MTP. They have no drive letter and no folder to mirror, so there is nothing for a drawer to list.
  • Remembering a name, colour or view per stick.
  • CD/DVD drives — removable USB volumes only.

Work overview

  • Rust: list drives and eject one — a small Windows module plus two commands the UI can call. (5 tasks)
  • Turning the drive list into drawers — the add/remove/rename logic, kept as a pure function with its own self-check, plus keeping these drawers out of the saved state. (5 tasks)
  • Interface — the eject button and the Settings switch. (2 tasks)
  • Verify — the full check, lint, build and Rust test sweep, then the plug-it-in pass by hand. (2 tasks)
